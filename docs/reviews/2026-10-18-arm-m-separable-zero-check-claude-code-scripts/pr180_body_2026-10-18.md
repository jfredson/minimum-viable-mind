## What was found, in plain words

Arm M's separable half at 10 million parameters takes its answer from the "this turn is yours" signal in the shared trunk, not from its built-in ownership answer. The trunk makes the model's own row of the lookup table about 3.8 times the size of the other rows, so even an even mix of the four rows gives the model's own answer (1,190 of 1,190). The row choice has gone flat: about a quarter on each agent (weight on the top row 0.26), whatever the built-in answer says. So swapping that answer changes nothing.

The row choice is flat because the marker-word embeddings, which both the built-in answer and the row keys are made from, shrank to 3.4 per cent of their start. Weight decay alone takes a weight to 33.5 per cent over this run (pull request 112's arithmetic), so training pushed them down. The row-choice weights themselves grew (1.9 and 1.4 times their start).

Take the acting signal out of the trunk only, keeping the built-in answer correct, and arm M's separable answers fall to chance (0.275). Arm T's stay at 0.978 and the toy arm M's at 1.000.

**Hypotheses** (stated in the method before any output): **H1** (the trunk carries the acting signal, so the built-in answer is not consulted) is **supported**. **H2** (the check is wired differently for arm M) is **rejected**: the swap moves arm M's slot on every action, through the same code path as arm T's. **H3** (a bookkeeping error over which items count as separable) is **rejected**: zero mismatches, and the returned answers are exactly the separable head's. **H4** (weight decay drove the row-choice weights to zero) is **rejected as stated**: those weights grew. What collapsed is the marker embeddings they read, about ten times further than decay alone could take them.

**Not settled:** why training shrank arm M's marker embeddings at 10 million and not arm T's or the toy's, and whether the trunk took over first or the row choice went flat first. Only final checkpoints exist; settling either would need training. One seed.
