## 1. Why neither reading works as a complete conclusion

### Reading 1 overclaims twice

First, **bookkeeping is not necessarily a mural**. A bookkeeping mechanism can be causally indispensable. A demonstrated effect on conversation or commitment tracking means something more than decorative self-description was involved, even if the best explanation is entirely ordinary computational control.

Your binary—decorative description versus constitutive centre—leaves out a large category:

> **A causally important representation that coordinates activity, without its phenomenal significance having been established.**

A navigation model can guide a vehicle. Its being a representation does not make it causally inert; its being causally important does not make it a subject.

Second, “nobody is home in the book’s sense” does not follow from the findings. To establish that, even conditionally on the identity wager, you would need to establish that the relevant organization is absent. Neither a failed probe nor an ambiguous ablation does that.

So **the mechanisms and their limitations are findings; the absence of an inside is not a finding. Confidence: high.**

### Reading 2 correctly identifies a vulnerability, but does not identify what was missed

The book treats the self as enacted organization. The experiments rely heavily on locating and manipulating an identifiable ownership representation. That is a genuine mismatch risk.

But a process can have stored state, and a stored representation can participate in an ongoing process. “Noun versus verb” is not itself a clean experimental distinction. Transplants and ablations can investigate processes when they target the right state variables, transitions or feedback pathways.

The more precise criticism is:

> The experiments have not established that their intervention targets capture the causally relevant organization, whether that organization is stored, reconstructed, distributed or redundant.

That supports redesign. It does not support announcing a hidden process-self.

**Confidence: high on the measurement limitation; low on whether these particular models possess the book’s proposed centre.**

## 3. The drafting session: mostly useful, but too quick about the human analogy

I agree with its diagnosis of drift: calibrating a transplant-based measure on engineered architectures is an instrumentation project, and its distance from the consciousness question must remain explicit.

I disagree with two parts.

### “Only possible with” does not require identity

Observation alone does not establish unrestricted necessity. But that is not peculiar to consciousness, and it does not mean the only available bridge is identity.

A theory could posit a **nonidentity law of dependence** under which a certain organization necessarily accompanies experience. One could then investigate the organization and assess the theory against available evidence. The resulting inference would remain theory-relative.

Likewise, empirical support does not require “ruling out every cheaper explanation.” That requirement would paralyze ordinary science. What matters is comparative explanatory and predictive performance, not exhaustive elimination.

The distinction is:

- **proving that a capacity cannot exist without experience**;
- **obtaining justified, defeasible evidence that the capacity indicates experience**.

The first is far stronger. The second is enough for a research programme.

### B is not yet a machine counterpart of the comparator model of agency

Human agency judgments partly depend on predictions derived from motor commands and comparisons with sensory consequences. They also depend on other prospective and retrospective information.

An actor signal saying “this turn is yours” is not yet an efference copy. A conversation log is not automatically one either. To establish the relevant functional analogy, you would want evidence of an internally generated action signal being used to predict consequences, followed by comparison and error correction.

Moreover, **sense of agency is not equivalent to consciousness or even to every form of experiential selfhood**. One can have experiences without experiencing oneself as their author.

The analogy supports asking a sharper question. It does not favor an experiential interpretation of B.

**Confidence: high on those distinctions.**

## 4. The reviewing session: stronger on the experimental record, but several claims are wrong

### A. “Identity or not at all” is too strong

For the reason above, identity is not the only possible bridge. A psychophysical dependence theory need not identify its two relata.

Also, declining philosophical zombies does not establish that *this particular structure* is the relevant one. One can accept organizational functionalism while rejecting owner-centred integration as the right criterion.

The reviewing session correctly identifies the live architectural questions. It overstates what must be assumed to investigate them.

### B. Selection matters—but does not erase behavioral evidence

This is a useful emphasis, not a new conclusion for me. My first answer already stressed that how the evidence was generated matters.

But these assertions go too far:

> “Human behaviour was never optimised to look conscious to other humans.”

Human social behavior has been shaped by evolution, development and social incentives, including incentives to communicate, conceal and simulate mental states. There is nevertheless an important difference between that history and training a model on human discourse and preferred responses. The difference needs describing accurately, not absolutely.

> “Behavioural evidence has been selected for and loses its weight.”

It can lose **discriminative weight**, sometimes severely. It does not automatically lose all weight. Its evidential value depends on how likely the behavior is under the competing explanations, given the training process.

> “Evidence that counts has to bypass the training objective.”

That is not a workable bright line. Internal structures are also shaped by training objectives. Unrewarded behaviors can be generalizations of rewarded policies.

In particular, **D does not clearly bypass training incentives**. Resisting unsupported user pressure while updating on evidence is closely related to behavior preference training commonly seeks to encourage. D can still be informative; it is not thereby an independent window onto an untrained interior.

**Confidence: high.**

### C. The characterization of attention schema theory is materially wrong

The reviewing session describes the theory as making a self-model “causally useful for reporting and routing and inert for the act.”

That is not a good description of Graziano’s attention schema theory. Its central proposal is that a simplified model of attention helps the system **control attention**, as well as supporting judgments and reports about awareness. The schema is not merely a causally idle commentary on an otherwise intact act.

Nor is an agent-identity or turn-tracking representation automatically an attention schema. These are different proposed variables.

Attention schema theory is worth considering. But **A does not specifically favor it merely because the intervention affects routing or conversation**. You would need evidence about representations of attention and their control functions.

**Confidence: high on the theoretical correction; low on whether attention schema theory explains the particular mechanism in A.**

### D. The proposed global-damage test still does not discriminate

The reviewer proposes:

> Bookkeeping damages only tasks needing the record; a centre degrades the whole act.

That distinction fails if many tasks depend on the record or on the routing mechanism it supports. Ordinary control machinery can produce widespread impairment. Conversely, a distributed centre could compensate and produce selective or modest impairment.

Thus:

**Broad damage is not a centre signature unless independently specified ordinary dependencies fail to explain its breadth.**

The proposed sham-record test is worth developing, but the crucial work is a causal model of which dependencies each account predicts. The intervention alone does not supply that model.

I agree that A’s router control cannot distinguish the two accounts **for the reported pattern**. I would not claim, without inspecting the entire protocol, that every possible result was barred from counting toward a centre.

### E. The caution about B is right—but even the proposed follow-up needs controls

The reviewer is right to demand evidence from successful solvers and to distinguish disrupting a signal from disrupting the record.

However, showing that a lookup-based solver fails when its lookup record is corrupted would be unsurprising. To advance the self-organization question, the test needs to separate:

- losing task-relevant information;
- losing provenance information;
- losing a coordination mechanism spanning multiple functions.

That is why I favor provenance-specific interventions, matched other-agent controls and rescue—not simply a larger performance collapse.

Think through the process experiment before spending on it. Its registration needs a result that distinguishes explicit causal accounts, not merely two interpretations of widespread damage.
