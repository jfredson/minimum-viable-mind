# The sensorimotor theory (O'Regan and Noë): notes on the draft records, 2026-11-21

Drafted by one working session for stage 3 of the consciousness-theories tracker. Everything
here comes from sources this session opened on 2026-11-21. Nothing here is in the repository.

## The core-claim source, and which copy was read

- **Source:** O'Regan and Noë, "A sensorimotor account of vision and visual consciousness",
  *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24(5), 2001 (source id `oregan-noe-2001`), as ruled by John.
- **Copy used:** the PDF on J. Kevin O'Regan's own website,
  https://kevin-oregan.net/OREGAN-NOE-BBS/ORegan%3BNoe.BBS.pdf. It is linked from the home page,
  and his old university address (nivea.psycho.univ-paris5.fr) now forwards to that site.
- **Differences from the journal text:** none expected. The copy is the journal's own typeset
  pages: every page has the journal's running head, and the pages run 939 to 1031, which takes in
  the target article, the other authors' commentaries and the authors' response. The only
  differences seen come from pulling the text out of the PDF (broken hyphens, stray spaces). The
  quotations repair those, and every quotation was checked against the extracted text.
- **Not used:** O'Regan 2023 (*Frontiers*), which widens the theory to all experience. None of
  the predictions needed it.

## How the core claims were chosen (repeatable)

I read the abstract and every section that states the theory itself: sections 1.3, 2 and 3,
section 6, and sections 8.4 to 9. From those I took each sentence the authors put as a
condition: what has to be true of a creature for it to see, or to be aware of what it sees.
Sentences that report data or answer objections were left out. The paper states six such
conditions. One sentence was quoted for each, in the paper's order:

1. `sm-c1`: mastering the patterns of how input changes with movement brings the experience of
   seeing (abstract).
2. `sm-c2`: there is no inner picture, and the world serves as its own picture (abstract).
3. `sm-c3`: the know-how must be in use now (section 3.2).
4. `sm-c4`: awareness also needs that know-how to be used in thinking and planning
   (section 2.6).
5. `sm-c5`: what makes the senses differ is their patterns, not their nerves (section 2.5).
6. `sm-c6`: seeing is not the same as any brain activity going on at that moment
   (section 8.4).

## Predictions (7), and which ones separate the theory from a rival

Every prediction is stated by the proponents, in the 2001 paper. Each core claim has at least
one prediction, and `sm-c1` has two (the colour experiment and the goggles). Only one
prediction separates the theory from a rival, judged by the rule that a rival's own opened
source must predict something different (ruling C):

- `sm-no-ncc` (core claim `sm-c6`) separates it from global workspace theory (`mashour-2020`),
  integrated information theory (`albantakis-2023`) and recurrent processing theory
  (`lamme-2018`). Each of those sources says a brain state or brain process is what experience
  is, or what it corresponds to. The 2001 paper says no brain state at a given moment matches
  an experience one to one.
- **Change blindness** (`sm-change-blindness`) and **missing unexpected objects**
  (`sm-inattentional`) are the theory's best-known predictions, and both could fail. But the
  rivals' own sources predict the same missed reports. Global workspace theory says conscious
  perception can be absent under inattention. Recurrent processing theory says people "will
  report not having seen" unattended changes. The rivals and this theory disagree only about
  what the misses mean: recurrent processing theory calls them "failures of attention and
  report, not of conscious vision". So under ruling C these two predictions do not separate the
  theories.
- The remaining four predictions (the colour experiment, inverting goggles, active control of a
  camera-to-touch device, and sensory substitution) could each fail. But no rival's opened
  source predicts something different for them, and a rival's silence does not count.

If the score script applies the ruled rules, testability would be 1 of 6 core claims, about 17.
The theory would fall below the bar of more than half, into "not yet defined sharply enough to
rank".

## Judgements: 8 in all

- 7 testability judgements (one per prediction). 5 are marked unsure.
- 1 commitment judgement (`com-bridgeman-sm-substitution`), read as "unclear" and marked unsure.
- 0 outcome judgements, because no commitment was read as "before".

## Every unsure judgement

- **`com-bridgeman-sm-substitution` (Bridgeman's study against the sensory-substitution
  prediction).** I read it as "unclear". The 2001 principle ("by changing these laws, it should
  be possible to change the locus of perception") came long before the study. But it sets no
  time scale, while the study ran for one hour. And in the same 2001 publication, the authors'
  response says phosphenes (the lights seen when the eye is pressed) are felt as sight because
  of patterns learned over a lifetime. A reader could instead say "before" and score the study
  against the theory: the abstract says the phosphene was "never adapting or changing modality
  as the sensorimotor theory predicts". Another reader could say "not-before". Only the
  abstract was read.
- **`tst-sm-colour-contingent` (the colour experiment).** It can fail only after enough
  training, and the theory does not say how much training is enough.
- **`tst-sm-active-control` (moving the camera yourself).** Integrated information theory's
  passage on intrinsic structure might be read as a contrary prediction. I read it as silent on
  this experiment.
- **`tst-sm-substitution` (sensory substitution).** The theory sets no amount of practice, and
  there is no agreed way to tell a user's report of "seeing" apart from working the answer out.
- **`tst-sm-change-blindness` (change blindness).** The theory and recurrent processing theory
  do disagree about whether the missed details were experienced. But their own sources agree on
  what people will report, which is the only thing measured, so this reading says the prediction
  does not separate them.
- **`tst-sm-no-ncc` (no brain state matches an experience one to one).** This is the one
  prediction that separates the theory from rivals. Whether it can really fail is doubtful: no
  study can show a match holds in every situation.

## Where a rule gave a result John may not have intended (applied anyway)

1. **The search finds almost nothing.** The required query matches papers that use the words
   "sensorimotor theory", "sensorimotor contingency" or "sensorimotor account" in the title or
   abstract. Europe PMC returns 9 such papers, and only 1 is a test. The experiments that bear
   most directly on the theory's predictions (sensory substitution, inverting goggles,
   eye-linked colour changes, change blindness) mostly do not name the theory in those words, so
   the search misses them. As a result the theory has no support score because of how the
   search was worded, not because nobody has tested what it predicts. A wider query, or a hand
   list built from the predictions, would change this.
2. **The theory's famous predictions do not separate it from rivals.** Change blindness is the
   result the theory is best known for predicting. The authors say so themselves ("an instance
   of how the theory made interesting predictions"). Under ruling C it scores nothing, because
   the rivals predict the same reports and differ only in what they think the reports mean. The
   rule measures differences in what people will observe; the real disagreement between these
   theories is about what was experienced, which no one has yet agreed how to measure.
3. **The authors say their data section is not a test.** Section 5.1 says the data "is not
   intended as a test of the theory in the everyday sense", and that a general framework cannot
   be put to "direct verification". The records still treat the passages as proponents' stated
   predictions, because each says what should be observed. The readers may want this caution
   in front of them.
4. **The one study counts against the theory only through its authors' own reading.** The
   Bridgeman study's prediction is its authors' reading of the theory. Rule D's dating test
   passes on dates alone (2001 before 2008). The decision therefore rests wholly on the
   commitment judgement, which here is a close call.
5. **Rival passages describing a position.** The recurrent processing theory passage comes from
   Lamme's 2018 paper, where he sets out two positions and does not, in that sentence, say "I
   hold". He is the proponent of the second position, which the pilot already treats as his
   theory's view (`lamme-2018`), so it is used here the same way.
