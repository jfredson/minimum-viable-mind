## The core-claim source

The ruled source is Seth, "Conscious artificial intelligence and biological naturalism",
*Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 49, e315, published online 2025-04-21. The journal version on
Cambridge Core shows only its abstract without a sign-in, so every quotation comes from the
**accepted manuscript** posted on PsyArXiv (preprint tz6an, version 2, 2025-04-22), which is headed
as accepted and "not yet copyedited and proofread". The verify list has an entry
(`v-seth-journal-text`) for checking the quotes against the copyedited text.

Version 1 of the preprint was created on OSF on 2024-06-30, but the file now attached to it is the
December 2024 submission. Five of the six core-claim sentences are in that December file word for
word; the seth-c3 sentence ("each and every conscious experience ...") is not, so it was added
between December 2024 and acceptance.

## Core claims: how they were chosen

Method, so it can be redone: read the whole paper; take sentences that say what the view holds
makes, or fails to make, a system conscious, from the paper's own summarising places first (the
abstract, the sentence it calls "the core claim", the two section summaries 4.5 and 5.8, and the
scenario section 5.5 that it says its case is "drawn towards"); leave out arguments against rivals,
claims about AI ethics, and passages that only report other people's ideas (Lane's mitochondria,
Levin's xenobots). Six claims came out:

1. seth-c1, the life claim (abstract), as John ruled.
2. seth-c2, conscious contents are the brain's best guesses (section 4.1, which calls it "the core
   claim from the perspective of consciousness"): the workings, as the ruling asks.
3. seth-c3, every experience brings together body and world signals in the service of keeping the
   body regulated (section 4.5 summary).
4. seth-c4, emotion and mood come from best guesses about the body (section 4.1). Hedged in the
   source ("may depend"); kept because it is where the workings meet the body, and where the
   proponents' testable predictions are.
5. seth-c5, consciousness is not computation, so a conscious machine would have to be living
   (section 5.5).
6. seth-c6, life is not enough: there can be life without consciousness (section 5.8).

Left out: "we are conscious creatures because we are living, flesh and blood 'beast machines'"
(section 4.5), because it restates seth-c1; the epistemic/ontological split in section 4.5, because
it is about how strongly to hold the view, not what it says.

## What the rules give, and where they may not do what John intended

- **Testability scores 0 and nothing is counted for support.** Six of eight predictions could fail,
  but none separates under ruling C, because no rival's own opened publication predicts anything
  about presence, body ownership, heartbeat sensing or the insula: the rivals are silent on the body,
  and silence does not count. The two predictions that do separate (from integrated information
  theory and attention schema theory, on whether non-living machines can be conscious) cannot fail
  today, because no agreed test for consciousness in a machine exists, as Seth himself says. So
  every core claim scores 0, the theory is below the bar, and the two outcomes below are recorded
  but count for nothing. The rule is applied as written. It may not be what John intended for a
  theory whose distinctive claims are about a domain its rivals ignore: ruling C then makes the
  theory look undefined, when the truth is that its rivals are. Worth a ruling.
- **The life claim is untestable by construction, today.** The ruled core claim (seth-c1) is the
  one with the clearest rival disagreement and no possible test. I read "could fail" as "could fail
  with methods that exist", and marked it unsure. Read as "in principle", seth-c1 and seth-c5 would
  each score 1 and the theory would reach 2 of 6 claims (33), still below the bar.
- **The dating test on the theory's own studies.** Suzuki 2013 was run by the authors of the 2012
  model. Rule D looks only at dates, so a 2012 prediction counts for their own 2013 study (at weight
  1, with no outside-camp protection). It came out against, which the rules would record as a
  failure that stands.
- **Rule D as briefed against the pilot's README.** The brief's dating rule (before the study's
  earliest public appearance) is what was applied. The pilot's README and the commitment header in
  `judgements.toml` still describe the superseded wording (before the data were collected).
  The `dates` tables here use `study_first_appeared`, as the brief asks. Neither study states a
  collection date, so the older wording would have made both commitments rest on the pilot's
  five-year convention instead (Jicol would still be "before"; Suzuki, 19 months, "unclear").

## Unsure judgements (every one)

1. `out-jicol-seth-presence-match` (mixed): the study measures stated expectations of emotion, a
   stand-in for the brain's predictions of body signals; the direction of mismatch mattered.
2. `out-suzuki-seth-low-interoception-more-illusion` (against): a second heartbeat-sensing method
   showed no relation; small sample; lifelike virtual hand.
3. `com-jicol-seth-presence-match` (before): whether the 2012 passage commits the view to this
   stand-in test.
4. `com-suzuki-seth-low-interoception-more-illusion` (before): the 2012 passage is hedged and
   offered as a reading of someone else's result.
5. `com-suzuki-seth-interoceptive-ownership` (unclear): the heartbeat-timing prediction is first
   written in the study that tests it.
6. `tst-seth-living-only` and 7. `tst-seth-ai-not-by-computation` (could not fail): the meaning of
   "could fail" when the only test does not exist yet; and for 7, whether Graziano's "believe it is
   conscious" is a claim about consciousness.
8. `tst-seth-feeling-from-prediction` (does not separate): Damasio's "directly ... an actual mixture
   of signals" leans against feelings-as-predictions but states no contrary prediction.
